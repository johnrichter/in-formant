# Warm-ups and vocal health

This page shows how to warm up your voice, which exercises help with which goal, and how to rest your voice after hard use.

**CAUTION.** This page is not medical advice. It does not replace a speech-language pathologist (SLP) or a doctor. Stop an exercise when you feel pain, tightness, or hoarseness. See a professional when a voice problem stays.

## How strong is the evidence?

The rankings below are a judgment. They come from small studies, from reviews of small studies, and from the advice of voice teachers. No study compares all these exercises with each other for a voice goal.

- The evidence is best for the effect of straw exercises on the effort of the voice. Even this evidence is small, and some results disagree.
- A search for this page found no study that tests these exercises for resonance or for a gender goal.
- A rank means "more likely to help, by this judgment". It does not mean "proven".
- The listener and your own body have the last word. When an exercise feels bad, stop it.

The list of sources is at the end of the page.

## Warm up

A warm-up prepares the voice for use. It takes 5 to 10 minutes. A good order:

1. Breathe in through the nose and out slowly, for one minute. Keep the shoulders and the neck loose.
2. Hum on a comfortable pitch for 30 seconds. Slide the pitch up and down in small steps.
3. Do two or three of the exercises below for 3 to 5 minutes. Use an easy voice and a soft start.
4. Say a few phrases at an easy pitch. Check the pitch track in InFormant. The line must look smooth, with few gaps.
5. Start your practice. Stay at an easy effort.

A warm-up must feel easy. Do not use it to push the voice to a high or a low pitch.

## Semi-occluded vocal tract exercises

A semi-occluded vocal tract exercise (SOVT) makes a small opening at the lips or in the mouth, and you make a sound through it. The opening raises the air pressure above the vocal folds. This pressure can make the folds vibrate with less effort.

A common pattern is to make the sound for 3 to 5 seconds, rest, and repeat 5 to 10 times. Keep the sound easy and steady. Stop when you feel strain or dizziness.

- **Straw phonation.** Make a sound through a thin straw. Slide the pitch up and down.
- **Cup bubbles.** Put the end of the straw a small depth under the water of a glass, and make a sound. This is straw phonation into water. The bubbles give a steady resistance, and you can see and hear them. A deeper straw gives more resistance, so start with a shallow depth.
- **Lip trill.** Let the lips flutter while you make a sound. Slide the pitch up and down. A lip trill needs steady air.
- **Tongue trill.** Roll the tip of the tongue, as in a rolled "r", and make a sound. Many people cannot do this at first. Do not force it.
- **Humming.** Close the lips and make a sound with a soft "m" or "n". Feel a buzz at the lips and the nose.
- **Voiced fricatives.** Make a sound with a buzzing consonant, such as "v", "z", or "zh". Hold it, and slide the pitch.

## What to look for in InFormant

- **Pitch track.** A good SOVT sound gives a smooth line with no gaps and no jumps by an octave. A line that breaks often means that the sound is pressed or uneven.
- **Pitch range.** A slide on a straw shows your range in a clean way. Use the pitch-only graph for this.
- **Spectrogram.** The harmonics must look clean. Noise between the harmonics means air leaks or strain.
- **Formants.** A straw or a trill changes the shape of the vocal tract. The formant tracks are not a good measure during these exercises. Check the formants again when you speak.

## Which exercise for which goal?

Each list goes from the exercise that is more likely to help to the exercise that is less likely to help. The reasons are short. All ranks are a judgment. See the section on evidence.

### Goal: an easier, less pressed voice (weight and the vocal folds)

1. **Cup bubbles.** Trials with people who have voice problems, and with older adults, tested straw phonation into water. They reported a better closure of the vocal folds and less breathiness.
2. **Straw phonation.** It gives much of the same resistance with no water. Studies report a lower pressure that the voice needs to start, and less effort.
3. **Lip trill.** Small studies show a lower contact of the vocal folds during the trill. The effect is short. It can help people who press the voice.
4. **Humming.** It has a mild resistance and is easy to do.
5. **Tongue trill.** It works like the lip trill, but it is harder to do.
6. **Voiced fricatives.** The resistance is small, so the effect is probably small.

These exercises ease a pressed voice. They do not set the weight of your voice in speech. Light and heavy qualities need their own practice with a coach.

### Goal: resonance (F1, F2, and F3)

No SOVT exercise trains resonance directly. This ranking tells you which exercise is easiest to use as a step toward resonance practice.

1. **Humming.** You can feel the buzz at the front of the mouth. You can move from a hum to a vowel with no break.
2. **Voiced fricatives.** The "v" and the "z" make you notice the position of the lips and the tongue.
3. **Lip trill.** The lips are loose, and the tract is short. You can move to a vowel after the trill.
4. **Tongue trill.** It needs the tongue at the front, so it makes you notice the tongue.
5. **Straw phonation.** The straw fixes the shape of the vocal tract, so the shape does not change.
6. **Cup bubbles.** The same reason as the straw. The water also limits the movement.

After the exercise, say a vowel at once, and look at F1, F2, and F3. The pages on feminization and masculinization show how.

### Goal: pitch (range and control)

1. **Lip trill.** Slides are easy, and a small study reported better pitch control after repeated practice.
2. **Straw phonation.** A slide on the straw is a clean way to find the edges of your range.
3. **Humming.** Slides are easy, and the sound is soft.
4. **Tongue trill.** Slides are possible, but the trill can stop at the ends of the range.
5. **Voiced fricatives.** The buzz can break at a high pitch.
6. **Cup bubbles.** The bubbles limit the slide. The sound stays at a small range.

### Goal: vocal health (a warm-up, a cool-down, and less effort)

1. **Straw phonation.** The evidence is best here. Studies report a lower pressure to start the voice and a lower effort.
2. **Cup bubbles.** Studies in voice therapy report good results. One study also reported more strain after a cup, so use a shallow depth and stop when you feel strain.
3. **Lip trill.** It is easy on the voice, and voice teachers use it a lot. The evidence is small.
4. **Humming.** It is gentle, and it is a good start and a good end.
5. **Tongue trill.** It is similar to the lip trill, but harder.
6. **Voiced fricatives.** They are gentle, but the evidence is the smallest.

## Recover and heal

A tired voice needs rest. Most mild cases of hoarseness after hard use clear in a few days with rest and water. These steps help:

- **Stop early.** Rest your voice at the first sign of pain, tightness, or hoarseness. A break of 5 to 10 minutes can help.
- **Talk less, and softly.** Use an easy voice. Do not shout.
- **Do not whisper.** Some sources advise against a whisper when the voice is tired. A soft, normal voice is the usual advice.
- **Do not clear the throat by force.** Swallow, or take a sip of water, or do a soft cough.
- **Drink water.** Sip through the day. Dry air, alcohol, and caffeine dry the throat.
- **Use gentle exercises.** After a rest, a few minutes of a soft hum or of straw phonation can help the voice to recover. Stop when you feel strain.
- **Sleep.** Rest helps the body to heal.

When to see a professional:

- Hoarseness that continues for more than 2 to 4 weeks.
- Pain when you speak or swallow, or a feeling of a lump in the throat.
- Blood in the sound or the saliva, or a sudden loss of the voice.
- A change of the voice that you cannot explain.
- A voice that you use for work and that tires every day.

Voice rest after an operation is different. Follow the plan of your surgeon and your SLP in that case. A complete voice rest is not the advice for a normal tired voice.

## Limits

- The studies are small. Many test one session, one measure, or a short course.
- The studies use different exercises, depths of water, and measures. The results do not always agree.
- InFormant measures sound. It does not see the vocal folds. Only a doctor can look at them.
